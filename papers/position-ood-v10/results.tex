\paragraph{Draft status.} Reference qualification, the 42-object primary prediction comparison, and its interval evaluation are complete. The nine-object acquisition comparison is also complete. Remaining material-shift and force-law MPM comparisons are in progress; their final tables will use complete declared cohorts.

\paragraph{Geometry and observations.}
The controlled object is a $240\times80\times80$\,mm deformable block with known canonical geometry, density $625$\,kg/m$^3$, and Poisson ratio $0.35$. Two position-controlled tools couple to unknown smooth Gaussian attachment footprints. The observed prefix contains three settled configurations: nominal tool poses, a 6\,mm right-tool translation along $y$, and a 6\,mm translation along $z$. A $640\times480$ rendered RGB-D marker pipeline provides the same 27 visible point positions to all methods. Known synthetic marker IDs establish correspondence; depth has independent 0.3\,mm Gaussian noise and image sampling introduces pixel quantization. The positional objective uses a common 0.3\,mm coordinate scale. No force, reaction, material, or attachment labels are supplied. Predictions are evaluated at 81 canonical query points, with visible and hidden-surface subsets reported separately.

\paragraph{Cohorts and shifts.}
The protocol declares nine development objects, twelve calibration objects, and three disjoint test cohorts: eighteen objects from the development parameter ranges, twelve with extrapolated elastic coefficients and spatial heterogeneity, and twelve with shifted environmental-field amplitude, center, width, and gravity direction. Material coefficients within development range from 8 to 40\,kPa, and total attachment stiffnesses from 1500 to 15000\,N/m. Each object has a new 3\,mm familiar-direction query, five new positional interaction patterns, and a doubled-amplitude query. New patterns include stretching, opposed tool motion, twisting, bending, and diagonal translation. An additional transfer challenge translates both tools together through the fixed spatial field. These are declared interaction and parameter shifts; the reference material law remains Neo-Hookean.

\paragraph{Unseen environmental force laws.}
A separately declared six-object extension tests anisotropic harmonic fields and signed two-Gaussian fields, with three objects per law and one per attachment layout. Both laws lie outside the single-Gaussian environmental family used by the inverse predictor. Each object supplies the same three prefix observations and is queried with the ten local actions and six whole-object translations. Materials, gravity direction, and compliant coupling remain unknown. All six predictors retain their frozen settings and learned weights; extension results are reported separately from the primary cohorts. All six reference fields pass the original numerical qualification criteria. The supplement gives the potentials and protocol.

\paragraph{Numerical references.}
Reference geometry uses quadratic finite elements and independent spatial and quadrature refinement. Every action must have a displacement RMS change below 20\,$\mu$m, equilibrium residual below $10^{-9}$\,N, positive deformation Jacobians above $0.1$, and reaction balance below $10^{-7}$\,N. Maximum-coordinate refinement changes are retained separately, since localized attachment-edge behavior converges more slowly. Acceptance in an RMS norm does not certify pointwise stress convergence. The inverse predictor uses a smaller discretization and an independently checked dense tangent implementation. A development-only sharp-attachment pilot motivated the finite smooth footprint: its localized compression reached the Jacobian guard under refinement, whereas the smooth version retained substantial nonrigid deformation and passed the original numerical thresholds.

\paragraph{Deformation content.}
All 87 reference fields pass the declared criteria: 63 independent development, calibration, and test objects, plus 24 spatial-transfer fields that reuse their physical parameters. The largest spatial-refinement RMS change is 19.34\,$\mu$m, the largest quadrature-refinement change is 0.278\,$\mu$m, and the smallest deformation Jacobian is 0.727. These checks qualify the displacement references used for evaluation.

Across all 42 test objects, the median object-average nonrigid coordinate RMS over the five new interaction patterns is 4.64\,mm, with an object range of 2.24--12.38\,mm. Relative to the already-deformed nominal configuration, the median nonrigid change induced by those actions is 0.89\,mm (range 0.64--1.19\,mm), after removing the best rigid transform between configurations. The benchmark therefore contains substantial shape change at both the configuration and interaction levels.

\paragraph{Comparisons.}
The physical baselines use official PhysTwin spring-mass and PhysGaussian material-point simulation components with documented adapters for the shared positional interface. They retain their own constitutive representations. PhysTwin learns per-spring parameters; the MPM adapter fits material variation, compliant tool coupling, and gravity direction. We tune initialization, discretization, and settling on development cases. A positional neural predictor is trained on a separate synthetic object bank, with an ID-action training variant and a separately reported variant exposed to all local action families. Controlled ablations fix environmental loading to its nominal value or freeze the spatial force at each material point's canonical position. The main predictor and neural comparisons cover all 42 declared test objects. The more expensive physical comparisons use the first six objects in each test cohort, giving two objects per attachment layout and an 18-object matched subset selected before test inference. Full Gaussian appearance reconstruction is outside this component benchmark.

\paragraph{Metrics and adaptation.}
The primary error is coordinate RMS displacement error,
\begin{equation}
 e=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{i=1}^N\|\hat u_i-u_i\|_2^2}.
\end{equation}
We also report Euclidean point RMSE, hidden-surface error, and residual motion after removing the best rigid transform. Incremental error compares $\hat u(a)-\hat u(a_0)$ against $u(a)-u(a_0)$, separating the predicted response to the new action from static shape-completion error. Aggregation treats the object as the independent unit. Paired comparisons resample objects rather than frames or points. Probing compares a fixed $+40$\,mm $y$ translation, a reproducible random action, and the sensitivity-based policy, all with one additional acquired response and a separate $-40$\,mm $y$ target. Twelve independent calibration objects set separate pre- and post-acquisition interval scales; the first nine familiar-parameter test objects evaluate transfer. A material-indexed-load ablation receives exactly the probe response selected for the spatial model. Before acquisition, the two neural baselines also predict all nine transfer objects; physical baselines reuse their original fits on the first six objects, with no additional observed responses.

\section{Prediction Results}
\paragraph{Generalization across interactions and physical shifts.}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports all 42 test objects. With only three positional observations, the spatial inverse achieves new-motion coordinate RMSE of $0.068$, $0.087$ and $0.067$\,mm for familiar parameters, material shifts and environmental shifts, respectively. Relative to the neural predictor trained on all local interaction families, the reductions are $43.1\%$, $78.3\%$ and $68.9\%$. The paired mean advantages are $0.051$, $0.315$ and $0.149$\,mm, with 95\% object-bootstrap intervals $[0.037,0.066]$, $[0.212,0.448]$ and $[0.066,0.276]$\,mm. Since all-action neural training includes these interaction families on disjoint objects, this comparison tests the benefit of per-object inference beyond action-family exposure.

The spatial inverse retains low error at doubled action amplitude ($0.071$--$0.089$\,mm). On material-shift objects, its incremental new-motion error is $0.020$\,mm versus $0.195$\,mm for all-action training, indicating improved response prediction beyond reconstruction of the nominal shape. These material shifts remain within the reference constitutive family. The remaining material-shift MPM comparisons are still being evaluated.

\paragraph{Matched physical component comparison.}
On the six predeclared environmental-shift objects, new-motion error is $0.065$\,mm for the spatial inverse, $0.303$\,mm for the PhysGaussian MPM adaptation and $1.061$\,mm for the PhysTwin core adaptation; the all-action neural baseline gives $0.297$\,mm on these same objects. The paired advantages over MPM and spring-mass adaptations are $0.238$ and $0.996$\,mm, with 95\% object-bootstrap intervals $[0.166,0.305]$ and $[0.811,1.189]$\,mm. Incremental-response errors are $0.031$, $0.208$ and $0.655$\,mm for the spatial inverse, MPM and spring-mass adaptations, respectively, showing a difference in response prediction beyond nominal-shape reconstruction. This comparison uses the common three-observation positional interface and documented development-selected settings. It concerns the adapted physical components, with the constitutive and environmental representations described above.

On the six matched familiar-parameter objects, new-motion errors are $0.075$, $0.310$ and $0.860$\,mm for the spatial inverse, MPM and PhysTwin adaptations, respectively. The paired advantage over MPM is $0.235$\,mm with 95\% interval $[0.169,0.302]$\,mm. On the six material-shift objects, the spatial inverse and PhysTwin adaptation give errors of $0.091$ and $1.495$\,mm, with paired advantage $1.404$\,mm and 95\% interval $[0.903,2.017]$\,mm. Their material-shift incremental-response errors are $0.021$ and $0.639$\,mm, respectively. These complete the three predeclared PhysTwin subsets; remaining MPM comparisons retain the same matched objects.

\paragraph{Accounting for the environment.}
On the same six environmental-shift objects, the nominal-environment ablation fixes gravity downward and omits the spatial field. Its new-motion error is $3.050$\,mm, versus $0.065$\,mm for the spatial inverse. Incremental-response error is $0.281$ versus $0.031$\,mm, so the benefit extends beyond correcting the nominal shape. This compares the declared ablation settings, including their regularization choices. With the same constitutive model, inverse resolution and regularization, material-indexed loading gives $0.075$\,mm on these environmental-shift objects; the spatial model has paired advantage $0.010$\,mm with 95\% interval $[0.001,0.019]$\,mm. On six familiar-parameter objects, the two are nearly tied ($0.075$\,mm spatial, $0.074$\,mm material-indexed). On the six material-shift objects, errors are $0.091$ and $0.096$\,mm, respectively, with paired advantage $0.005$\,mm and interval $[-0.0001,0.0114]$\,mm. Thus all three local-query cohorts show only a modest distinction between these coordinate-frame explanations.

\paragraph{Predictive interval coverage.}
With one interval scale fixed on twelve independent calibration objects, average held-coordinate coverage is $98.3\%$, $95.9\%$ and $98.7\%$ for familiar parameters, material shifts and environmental shifts, respectively. Mean half-widths are $0.169$, $0.168$ and $0.181$\,mm. At least $90\%$ of held coordinates are covered for $18/18$, $10/12$ and $12/12$ objects, respectively; the lowest material-shift coverage is $88.9\%$. These results use all seven held local actions per object and the unchanged calibration scale. They measure predictive uncertainty before acquisition, separately from material-parameter identifiability and post-probe performance. The supplement reports coverage and width together.

\paragraph{Whole-object translation before acquisition.}
Across six translations on all nine declared transfer objects, coordinate RMSE is $0.183$\,mm for the spatial inverse and $0.141$\,mm for material-indexed loading, versus $11.436$ and $16.514$\,mm for all-action and ID-action neural training. Neither neural variant was trained on these large global translations. The paired spatial-model advantage over material-indexed loading is $-0.042$\,mm with 95\% interval $[-0.110,0.039]$\,mm. On the separately held $-40$\,mm $y$ target, errors are $0.314$ and $0.248$\,mm, respectively. Thus mechanics supports strong positional transfer, but three local observations do not establish a predictive advantage for the spatial coordinate choice before acquisition. The independently calibrated pre-acquisition intervals cover $81.9\%$ of held-target coordinates with mean half-width $0.373$\,mm; $5/9$ objects reach $90\%$ coverage. The post-probe comparison evaluates an additional response through prediction error, coverage and width together.

On the six predeclared physical-baseline transfer objects (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}), translation errors are $0.194$, $0.368$ and $0.804$\,mm for the spatial inverse, MPM and PhysTwin adaptations, respectively. Paired advantages over MPM and PhysTwin are $0.175$ and $0.610$\,mm, with 95\% intervals $[0.076,0.274]$ and $[0.423,0.799]$\,mm. Held-target errors are $0.330$, $0.533$ and $0.830$\,mm, respectively. Every model reuses its original three-observation fit. This subset comparison uses no newly acquired responses and is separate from the nine-object acquisition study.

\paragraph{Using the same additional positional response.}
The complete nine-object matched-frame comparison separates the value of the environmental representation from probe selection. After observing the same additional response, held-target error falls from $0.314$ to $0.084$\,mm for the spatial model, while material-indexed loading changes from $0.248$ to $0.297$\,mm. The post-acquisition paired advantage is $0.213$\,mm with 95\% object-bootstrap interval $[0.057,0.409]$\,mm. The spatial model can use the new observation to improve predictions across the fixed world field; a material-indexed explanation cannot reproduce that position dependence. All nine sensitivity-based choices equal the fixed $+40$\,mm $y$ probe. This result supports the representation's use of additional evidence, while the adaptive and fixed policies make identical acquisitions on these objects.

\paragraph{One-response acquisition and predictive uncertainty.}
Table~\ref{tab:active} and Figure~\ref{fig:acquisition} report all nine objects. Fixed and sensitivity-based probing reduce held-target error by $73.2\%$, from $0.314$ to $0.084$\,mm; reproducible random probing gives $0.163$\,mm. Their paired advantage over random probing is $0.079$\,mm with 95\% object-bootstrap interval $[0.013,0.151]$\,mm. The fixed probe is a strong mirror of the held target, and the sensitivity policy selects it for every object, yielding identical predictions. The experiment therefore demonstrates useful one-response adaptation and an advantage over these random draws, without an advantage over the fixed choice.

With policy-specific scales frozen on twelve independent calibration objects, fixed/adaptive probing raises mean coordinate coverage from $81.9\%$ to $92.0\%$ while reducing mean interval half-width from $0.373$ to $0.157$\,mm. Random probing gives $88.1\%$ coverage and $0.238$\,mm half-width. Six of nine objects reach $90\%$ coordinate coverage after each policy, compared with five before acquisition. Thus narrower intervals accompany improved aggregate coverage and prediction, although object-level coverage remains uneven in this small transfer cohort.

\paragraph{Prediction under unseen force laws.}
The separate six-object extension replaces the reference field with an anisotropic harmonic potential or a signed two-Gaussian potential, while retaining the frozen single-Gaussian inverse model and original three observations. Across three objects per law, spatial-inverse new-motion errors are $0.082$ and $0.068$\,mm, versus $0.136$ and $0.139$\,mm for all-action neural training. Whole-object translation errors are $0.392$ and $0.267$\,mm for the spatial inverse, compared with $0.665$ and $0.249$\,mm for material-indexed loading. Thus the spatial representation helps under harmonic loading in these cases, while the two-Gaussian cases slightly favor material-indexed loading. The all-action neural errors are $11.041$ and $11.413$\,mm on these translations. The PhysTwin core adaptation gives new-motion errors of $0.733$ and $0.864$\,mm and translation errors of $0.945$ and $0.773$\,mm for the two laws, respectively. These are small, predeclared force-law groups; the accompanying report retains each object's result. The MPM comparison is still being completed.
